## Part 2: Knowledge Points

### 6.1 The Earth and the Solar System

#### 6.1.1 The Earth (Core + Supplement)

**Core**
- The Earth is a planet that rotates on its **tilted axis** once every **approximately 24 hours**.
  - The Earth is opaque, so only the half facing the Sun is illuminated; rotation carries each place into and out of the illuminated half → **day and night**;
  - Rotation also causes the **apparent daily motion of the Sun** (rising in the east and setting in the west).
- The Earth orbits the Sun once every **approximately 365 days**; this explains the **periodic nature of the seasons**.
  - The tilt of the axis changes the Sun's **altitude** and the **length of the day** during the year → different amounts of solar radiation are received in different seasons.
- The Moon orbits the Earth once every **approximately one month**; this explains the **periodic cycle of the Moon's phases**.
  - The Moon does not emit light; we see the half illuminated by the Sun. As the Sun–Earth–Moon positions change, the fraction of the illuminated half that we can see changes → moon phases.

**Supplement**
- **Average orbital speed**: `v = 2πr / t`
  - `r` = average orbital radius (distance from the centre of the central body);
  - `t` = orbital period;
  - be able to define and use the equation.

**Common traps**
- Day/night is caused by **rotation**; seasons are caused by **revolution + axial tilt**, not by changing distance from the Sun.
- Seasons and moon phases are **Core**, not Supplement.
- In `v = 2πr/t`, r must be the distance from the **centre** of the central body; add the radius of the central body if an altitude is given.

#### 6.1.2 The Solar System (Core + Supplement)

**Core**
- The Solar System contains:
  - **one star, the Sun**;
  - **eight named planets**, in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune;
  - **minor planets**, including **dwarf planets** such as Pluto and **asteroids** in the asteroid belt;
  - **moons** that orbit the planets;
  - smaller bodies, including **comets** and natural satellites.
- The four planets nearest the Sun are **rocky and small**; the four furthest from the Sun are **gaseous and large**. This difference is explained by the **accretion model** of Solar System formation, including:
  - the model's dependence on **gravity**;
  - the presence of many elements in interstellar clouds of gas and dust;
  - the **rotation** of material in the cloud and the formation of an **accretion disc**.
- The strength of the gravitational field at a planet's surface depends on the planet's **mass**; it decreases as the distance from the planet increases.
- Calculate the time for light to travel a significant distance, e.g. between objects in the Solar System: `t = d / c`, where `c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s`.
- The Sun contains **most of the mass** of the Solar System; this explains why the planets orbit the Sun.
- The force that keeps an object in orbit around the Sun is the **gravitational attraction of the Sun**.

**Supplement**
- Planets, minor planets and comets have **elliptical orbits**; the Sun is **not at the centre** of the ellipse (except when the orbit is approximately circular).
- Analyse and interpret planetary data: orbital distance, orbital duration, density, surface temperature and gravitational field strength at the surface.
- As distance from the Sun increases: the Sun's gravitational field becomes weaker and the planets' **orbital speeds decrease**.
- An object in an elliptical orbit travels **faster when it is closer to the Sun**; this can be explained by **conservation of energy** (lower potential energy, higher kinetic energy near the Sun).

**Common traps**
- A light-year is a unit of **distance**, not time.
- The Sun is at one **focus** of the ellipse, not at the centre; only near-circular orbits are approximately centred.
- The inner four planets are **rocky**; the outer four are **gaseous giants**.
- The gravitational force provides the centripetal force; do not call it "centrifugal force".

### 6.2 Stars and the Universe

#### 6.2.1 The Sun as a Star (Core + Supplement)

**Core**
- The Sun is a star of **medium size**, consisting mostly of **hydrogen and helium**.
- It radiates most of its energy in the **infrared, visible and ultraviolet** regions of the electromagnetic spectrum.

**Supplement**
- Stars are powered by **nuclear reactions** that release energy; in stable stars the reactions involve the **fusion of hydrogen into helium**.

**Common traps**
- The Sun is not "burning" chemically; it is powered by **nuclear fusion**.
- The Sun is a **medium-sized** star, not the largest or smallest.

#### 6.2.2 Stars (Core + Supplement)

**Core**
- Galaxies are each made up of **many billions of stars**.
- The Sun is a star in the galaxy known as the **Milky Way**.
- Other stars in the Milky Way are **much further from Earth** than the Sun is.
- Astronomical distances are measured in **light-years**: one light-year is the distance travelled by light in a vacuum in **one year**.

**Supplement**
- `1 light-year = 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m`.
- **Life cycle of a star**:
  1. A star is formed from **interstellar clouds of gas and dust** containing hydrogen.
  2. A **protostar** is a collapsing cloud that increases in temperature because of its internal gravitational attraction.
  3. The protostar becomes a **stable star** when the inward gravitational force is balanced by the outward force due to the high temperature in its centre.
  4. All stars eventually **run out of hydrogen** as fuel for the nuclear reactions.
  5. Most stars expand to form **red giants**; more massive stars expand to form **red supergiants** when most of the hydrogen in the centre has been converted to helium.
  6. A red giant from a less massive star forms a **planetary nebula** with a **white dwarf** at its centre.
  7. A red supergiant explodes as a **supernova**, forming a nebula containing hydrogen and new heavier elements, and leaving a **neutron star** or a **black hole** at its centre.
  8. The nebula from a supernova may form **new stars with orbiting planets**.

**Common traps**
- A protostar is not yet a stable star; stability requires gravity to be balanced by thermal pressure.
- Low-mass star: red giant → planetary nebula + white dwarf. High-mass star: red supergiant → supernova → neutron star/black hole.
- Supernovae scatter **heavy elements** into space, providing material for the next generation of stars and planets.

#### 6.2.3 The Universe (Core + Supplement)

**Core**
- The Milky Way is one of **many billions of galaxies** making up the Universe; its diameter is approximately **100 000 light-years**.
- **Redshift**: an increase in the observed **wavelength** of electromagnetic radiation emitted from **receding** stars and galaxies.
- Light emitted from distant galaxies appears **redshifted** compared with light emitted on Earth.
- Redshift is evidence that the **Universe is expanding** and supports the **Big Bang theory**.

**Supplement**
- **Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR)**: microwave radiation of a specific frequency observed at all points in space around us.
- The CMBR was produced **shortly after the Universe was formed**; as the Universe expanded, this radiation was stretched into the **microwave** region of the electromagnetic spectrum.
- The **speed** at which a galaxy is moving away from Earth can be found from the **change in wavelength** of its starlight due to redshift.
- The **distance** of a far galaxy can be determined using the **brightness of a supernova** in that galaxy.
- **Hubble constant H₀**: the ratio of the speed at which a galaxy is moving away from Earth to its distance from Earth: `H₀ = v / d`.
- The current estimate is `H₀ = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹`.
- `d / v = 1 / H₀` represents an estimate for the **age of the Universe**; this supports the idea that all matter in the Universe was present at a **single point**.

**Common traps**
- Redshift means the **wavelength increases**; it is not simply "the colour becomes red".
- The unit of H₀ is **s⁻¹**, not km/s.
- The age of the Universe is approximately **1/H₀**, not H₀.
- The CMBR does not come from a particular star; it is the "afterglow" of the early Universe.

## Part 2.5: Intuitive Understanding (for memory, not examined)

- **Day/night vs seasons**: day/night is like **spinning on the spot** (rotation); seasons are like **walking around a circle while tilted** (revolution + axial tilt), not "being closer to the fire".
- **Moon phases**: the Moon does not emit light; we only see the half illuminated by the Sun. As the Moon orbits Earth, the fraction of that illuminated half we can see changes → phases.
- **Solar System formation = spinning pizza dough**: a cloud of gas and dust contracts under gravity, spins faster and flattens into an **accretion disc**; the centre ignites as the Sun, and the "toppings" in the disc clump into planets.
- **Life of a star = tug of war**: gravity pulls inwards, thermal pressure from fusion pushes outwards. When the hydrogen runs out, the balance fails → red giant/red supergiant → white dwarf or supernova.
- **Redshift = an ambulance moving away**: as a siren moves away, the pitch drops (wavelength increases); as a light source moves away, the wavelength increases (redshift). The farther a galaxy is, the faster it recedes and the greater its redshift.
- **Expanding Universe = raisin bread**: as the dough rises, every raisin moves away from every other raisin; it is space itself stretching, not galaxies flying through space.
- **Light-year = a distance**: not "one year", but the distance light travels in one year (about 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m).
- **H₀ and the age of the Universe**: H₀ is "how fast it is expanding now"; its reciprocal `1/H₀` is "roughly how long it has been expanding".

> The following values are beyond the syllabus, for understanding only – do not memorise: axial tilt about 23.5°; Earth's orbit about 365.25 days; Moon's orbit about 27.3 days and phase cycle about 29.5 days; Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago; the Sun is about 73% hydrogen and 25% helium; core temperature about 15 million °C; about 8 solar masses separates low- and high-mass stars; iron fusion absorbs energy; black dwarfs; a remnant of about 3 solar masses forms a neutron star; the CMBR was emitted about 380 000 years after the Big Bang; the Universe is about 13.8 billion years old.

## Part 5: Paper 4 Answer Templates

- **Day/night**: The Earth rotates on its tilted axis once about every 24 hours, so the side facing the Sun is in daylight while the other side is in darkness.
- **Seasons**: The Earth orbits the Sun once about every 365 days; because its axis is tilted, the angle at which sunlight strikes each hemisphere changes during the year, producing the seasons.
- **Moon phases**: The Moon orbits the Earth about once a month; the changing relative positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon change the fraction of the Moon's illuminated half that we can see.
- **Orbital speed**: For a nearly circular orbit, average orbital speed `v = 2πr / t`, where r is the orbital radius and t is the orbital period.
- **Solar System formation**: A cloud of gas and dust collapsed under gravity, rotated and formed an accretion disc; the inner region was hot, so only rocky materials condensed to form small rocky planets, while the outer region was cold enough for gases to accumulate and form large gas giants.
- **Light travel time**: `t = d / c`, where c is the speed of light.
- **Elliptical orbit**: A body moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun because gravitational potential energy is smallest and kinetic energy is largest (energy is conserved).
- **Life cycle of a star**: A nebula collapses to form a protostar; when gravity is balanced by thermal pressure from hydrogen fusion, it becomes a stable star. When the hydrogen runs out, a low-mass star becomes a red giant and then a planetary nebula with a white dwarf, while a high-mass star becomes a red supergiant and then a supernova, leaving a neutron star or black hole.
- **Redshift**: Light from a receding galaxy has a longer observed wavelength (redshift); this is evidence that the Universe is expanding and supports the Big Bang theory.
- **Hubble's law**: `v = H₀d`; the Hubble constant is the ratio of recession speed to distance, and `1/H₀` gives an estimate of the age of the Universe.
- **CMBR**: Cosmic microwave background radiation is microwave radiation observed in all directions; it was produced shortly after the Universe formed and has been stretched into the microwave region as the Universe expanded.
